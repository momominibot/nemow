Your Postpartum Journey

A gentle, evidence-based guide to recovery — physical, emotional, and nutritional. Take it one day at a time. You grew a human; now let yourself heal.

schedule What to expect, week by week

Every birth and every body is different. This timeline is a general guide — your recovery may be faster or slower. Be gentle with yourself.

healing Vaginal birth recovery

  • Perineal care: Use a peri bottle with warm water after every bathroom visit. Pat dry — don't rub. Witch hazel pads or cold compresses can ease swelling in the first few days.
  • Sitz baths: Warm shallow baths 2-3 times daily for the first week can soothe soreness and promote healing. Keep the area clean and dry between.
  • Pain management: Over-the-counter pain relief as recommended by your provider. Ice packs wrapped in cloth for the first 24-48 hours, then warm compresses.
  • Stools: Drink plenty of water and eat fiber-rich foods. A stool softener may be recommended to avoid straining. Don't hold your breath when pushing — exhale.

medical_services C-section recovery

  • Incision care: Keep the incision clean and dry. Follow your surgeon's instructions for dressing changes. Watch for redness, swelling, discharge, or separation.
  • Movement: Start walking as soon as you're able (usually within 24 hours) to prevent blood clots. Avoid lifting anything heavier than your baby for 6-8 weeks.
  • Pain: Incision pain is normal and gradually improves. Use prescribed pain medication as directed. Coughing or laughing may hurt — hold a pillow against your abdomen for support.
  • Recovery is longer: A C-section is major abdominal surgery. Full recovery typically takes 8-12 weeks. Don't rush — internal healing takes longer than the skin incision.

accessibility Pelvic floor health

Your pelvic floor went through a lot. Whether vaginal or C-section, these muscles need attention.

  • Kegels: Gently contract and release the muscles you use to stop urine flow. Start with 3 sets of 10 daily, but only after any tears have healed. Quality matters more than quantity — fully relax between contractions.
  • Leakage: A small amount of stress incontinence (leaking when coughing/laughing) is common in the first weeks. If it persists beyond 6 weeks, see a pelvic floor physiotherapist — it's treatable.
  • Pain during sex: Common due to hormonal changes and scar tissue. Use lubricant, go slow, communicate with your partner. If pain persists, see your provider or pelvic floor PT.

restaurant Eating to nourish & heal

Breastfeeding burns approximately 300-500 extra calories per day. You don't need to "eat for two" anymore, but you do need nutrient-dense food to support milk production and your own recovery. i

🥩 Protein 71g+ daily. Salmon, eggs, Greek yogurt, lentils, chicken, tofu. Supports tissue repair and milk protein.
🥬 Iron-rich Spinach, red meat, lentils, pumpkin seeds. Pair with vitamin C (citrus, bell peppers) for better absorption.
🥑 Healthy fats Avocado, nuts, olive oil, fatty fish (salmon, sardines). DHA supports baby's brain development via breast milk.
💧 Hydration Drink to thirst — typically 2.5-3L daily. A glass of water at every feeding session. Milk is ~87% water.
🌾 Whole grains Oats, brown rice, quinoa. Oats are a traditional galactagogue (milk-supporting food) and provide sustained energy.
🫐 Calcium & vitamin D 1000mg calcium daily (dairy, fortified plant milks, sesame, leafy greens). 600 IU vitamin D. Your bones need replenishing.

local_cafe Galactagogues (milk-supporting foods)

These foods and herbs have traditional use for supporting milk supply. Evidence is mixed but they're generally safe when consumed as food:

  • Oats: The most widely recommended. A bowl of oatmeal daily may help — also provides iron and fiber.
  • Fenugreek: The most studied galactagogue herb. Can increase supply in some people, but may cause GI upset in baby. Use short-term (2-3 weeks) and stop if supply doesn't improve.
  • Brewer's yeast: Rich in B vitamins and protein. Often mixed into smoothies or lactation cookies.
  • Fennel & blessed thistle: Often combined. May help with let-down reflex.
  • Dark leafy greens & alfalfa: Nutrient-dense and traditionally used.
Important

The most effective way to build and maintain milk supply is frequent, effective milk removal — feed or pump 8-12 times in 24 hours, especially in the first 6 weeks. Galactagogues support but don't replace this. If supply is a concern, see a lactation consultant (IBCLC).

no_food Foods & substances to limit

  • Alcohol: If you choose to drink, wait 2-3 hours per standard drink before breastfeeding. "Pump and dump" doesn't speed elimination — alcohol leaves milk as it leaves your bloodstream.
  • Caffeine: Up to 300mg daily (about 2-3 cups of coffee) is generally considered safe. Some babies are sensitive — watch for fussiness or poor sleep.
  • High-mercury fish: Avoid swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish. Choose low-mercury options like salmon, sardines, cod.
  • Gassy foods (if baby is fussy): Some babies react to cruciferous veggies (broccoli, cabbage), dairy, or soy in your diet. This is individual — don't eliminate foods unless you notice a clear pattern.

self_improvement Your mental health matters

Postpartum mood changes are common. It's normal to feel overwhelmed, tearful, or anxious in the first weeks. But there's a difference between "baby blues" and something that needs support.

Baby blues (normal)

Affects up to 80% of new parents. Starts 2-3 days after birth, peaks around day 5, resolves by day 10-14. Symptoms: mood swings, tearfulness, irritability, anxiety, trouble sleeping even when baby sleeps. This is hormonal and temporary.

Postpartum depression (needs support)

Affects 1 in 7 women. Can start any time in the first year. Symptoms: persistent sadness, loss of interest, feelings of worthlessness or guilt, changes in appetite/sleep beyond baby's needs, difficulty bonding with baby, thoughts of harming yourself or baby. This is not your fault and it is treatable. Talk to your doctor, midwife, or a mental health professional. i

Question 10 asks about self-harm thoughts. If you score anything other than 0 on question 10, seek help immediately. In Singapore: SOS 1-767 (24/7), text 741741, or go to the nearest emergency department. You can also ask your partner, family, or friend to help you get support.

Self-care that actually helps

• Sleep when baby sleeps — housework can wait
• Accept every offer of help — meals, errands, holding baby
• Step outside for 10 minutes daily — fresh air and sunlight help mood
• Connect with other new parents — isolation worsens symptoms
• Talk to your partner about how you're really feeling
• Therapy works — CBT and interpersonal therapy are evidence-based for PPD
• Medication is safe while breastfeeding for many people — discuss with your doctor

groups Postpartum anxiety & OCD

Depression isn't the only postpartum mood disorder. Anxiety is actually more common:

  • Postpartum anxiety: Constant worry, racing thoughts, feeling on edge, physical symptoms (heart racing, shortness of breath), difficulty sleeping even when baby sleeps. Affects ~10-15% of new parents.
  • Postpartum OCD: Intrusive, unwanted thoughts about the baby (often frightening — e.g., "what if I drop the baby?"), followed by compulsive behaviors (checking, rechecking, avoiding). These thoughts are ego-dystonic (you don't want them) and you would never act on them. Affects ~3-5%. Very treatable with therapy.
  • Postpartum psychosis (rare, emergency): Onset within first 2 weeks — confusion, delusions, hallucinations, extreme mood swings, inability to care for self or baby. This is a psychiatric emergency. Call emergency services or go to the ER immediately. Affects 1-2 per 1000 births.

fitness_center Returning to movement

Exercise after birth is gradual. Always get medical clearance at your 6-week checkup (or 8 weeks for C-section) before starting structured exercise. Before that, gentle walking is fine if you feel up to it.

directions_walk
0–6 weeks: Gentle walking & breathwork Short walks around the house or block, as tolerated. Diaphragmatic breathing and gentle pelvic floor awareness. No crunches, planks, or high-impact. Listen to your body — if bleeding increases or you feel pain, rest. i
self_improvement
6–12 weeks: Core & pelvic floor rebuild With clearance: pelvic floor exercises (Kegels with full relaxation), transverse abdominis activation, gentle yoga or Pilates (modified), brisk walking. Avoid crunches, sit-ups, planks, and high-impact until diastasis recti has improved. Focus on form, not intensity.
fitness_center
3–6 months: Gradual intensity Can add low-impact cardio (swimming, cycling, elliptical), light strength training, modified Pilates/yoga. If diastasis recti has closed, can gradually introduce planks and more advanced core work. Watch for pelvic floor symptoms (leakage, heaviness, pain) — back off if they occur.
bolt
6+ months: Return to pre-pregnancy activity Most people can return to their pre-pregnancy exercise routine by 6 months, though some take longer. Running, HIIT, heavy lifting — all possible if your pelvic floor is strong and you've built up gradually. If you have persistent leakage, pain, or pelvic heaviness, see a pelvic floor physiotherapist before high-impact exercise.

warning What to avoid in the first 6 months

  • Crunches & sit-ups: Worsen diastasis recti and put pressure on the pelvic floor.
  • Planks (until cleared): Can increase intra-abdominal pressure and worsen separation.
  • High-impact exercise: Running, jumping jacks, burpees — too much for healing pelvic floor and joints (relaxin may still be present, especially if breastfeeding).
  • Heavy lifting: Nothing heavier than your baby for 6-8 weeks, especially after C-section.
  • Exercising through pain: Pain is a signal to stop. Discomfort from muscle soreness is different from sharp pain, pelvic heaviness, or increased bleeding.
Diastasis recti (abdominal separation)

Very common after pregnancy — the connective tissue (linea alba) stretches to make room. Most improve within 6-12 months with proper core rehab. Check by lying on your back, knees bent, and lifting your head slightly — feel for a gap above/below the belly button. A gap of 1-2 finger-widths is common. More than 2-3 fingers, or a persistent bulge, may benefit from physical therapy. Avoid exercises that increase intra-abdominal pressure (crunches, planks) until it improves.

favorite Sex & intimacy after birth

There's no "right" time to resume sex. Most providers advise waiting until your 6-week checkup (or 8 weeks for C-section) to ensure healing is complete. But physical readiness ≠ emotional readiness — both matter.

  • Pain is common at first: Hormonal changes (especially if breastfeeding) cause vaginal dryness and thinning. Use a water-based lubricant generously. Go slow, communicate, and stop if it hurts.
  • Desire may be low: Fatigue, body image changes, hormonal shifts, and the demands of a newborn all affect libido. This is normal and usually improves over time. Non-sexual intimacy (cuddling, massage, holding hands) matters too.
  • Breastfeeding changes things: Lower estrogen can cause dryness and lower desire. This resolves for most people after weaning or as cycles return.
  • Contraception: You can get pregnant before your first period returns — even while exclusively breastfeeding. Discuss contraception with your provider at the 6-week visit. Options include condoms, IUD, implant, progestin-only pills (safe for breastfeeding).

water_drop When will my period return?

This varies widely and depends mostly on breastfeeding patterns:

🍼 Exclusively breastfeeding Periods often return 6+ months postpartum, sometimes not until weaning. Night feedings especially suppress ovulation. But it's not reliable contraception — ovulation happens before the first period.
🍼 Mixed feeding Periods typically return 3-6 months postpartum as formula supplementation increases and prolactin levels drop.
🍼 Formula only Periods usually return 6-8 weeks postpartum, sometimes as early as 4 weeks. Your cycle may be irregular for the first few months.
⚠️ First periods May be heavier, more painful, or irregular than your pre-pregnancy cycles. This usually normalizes within 3-6 cycles. If bleeding is extremely heavy (soaking a pad in 1 hour) or very painful, see your provider.
Your cycle after baby

Many people find their cycles change after pregnancy — some have lighter periods, some heavier, some more or less painful. This is normal as your body adjusts. If you're tracking your cycle in Ovuluna, the first 3-6 postpartum cycles may be irregular — that's expected data, not a problem. Your body is recalibrating. i

emergency When to call your doctor immediately

These symptoms require urgent medical attention. Don't wait — call your provider, go to the emergency department, or call emergency services.

bloodtype Postpartum hemorrhage warning

  • Soaking a maxi pad in 1 hour or less (for 2+ hours)
  • Passing clots larger than a golf ball / plum
  • Bleeding that gets heavier instead of lighter after day 3-4
  • Dizziness, fainting, rapid heartbeat, pale/clammy skin

coronavirus Infection warning

  • Fever over 38°C (100.4°F) — especially after the first 24 hours
  • Chills, body aches, feeling very unwell
  • Foul-smelling vaginal discharge
  • C-section incision: redness, swelling, warmth, pus, or opening
  • Perineal tear: increasing pain, swelling, or discharge
  • Painful, red, warm area on the breast (mastitis) — especially with fever
  • Painful urination, urgency, or blood in urine (UTI)

heart_plus Cardiovascular & clot warning

  • Chest pain, shortness of breath, or heart palpitations that don't resolve
  • Pain, swelling, redness, or warmth in one leg (deep vein thrombosis)
  • Sudden severe headache, vision changes, or confusion (could be preeclampsia or stroke)
  • Seizures

psychology Mental health emergency

  • Thoughts of harming yourself or your baby — call emergency services immediately
  • Confusion, disorientation, or not knowing where you are
  • Seeing or hearing things that aren't there (hallucinations)
  • Extreme paranoia or delusions
  • Inability to care for yourself or your baby

When in doubt, call

You know your body best. If something feels wrong — even if it's not on this list — call your provider. It's always better to check. Postpartum complications can develop rapidly, and early treatment makes a big difference. You are not overreacting. You are advocating for yourself, and that's exactly what a good parent does.
